Mouse DPI Test

Slides your mouse a measured distance and counts the pixels it moves. The result estimates your mouse’s sensitivity (DPI / CPI).

How to measure your DPI

  1. Disable pointer acceleration (see the notice in the test below). Otherwise the estimate is meaningless.
  2. Pick a drag length — 3 inches (7.5 cm) is a good balance of accuracy and space.
  3. Lay a ruler or measuring tape on your mousepad. Press and hold anywhere in the ruler below, move your mouse exactly the length you picked — measured in the real world, on the desk — in a straight lateral line, then release inside the page.
  4. Read the estimate and compare against the common DPI presets shown above your result.
The selected length must be followed in real life. The ruler’s on-screen span between the Start and End markers is only a proportional approximation of your chosen length. For an accurate result, measure the distance physically with a ruler or tape on your mousepad — trust the tape, not the screen markers.
The result collapses to your monitor’s PPI if you drag marker-to-marker. Browsers only report cursor pixels, never physical distance. If you follow the on-screen markers instead of a measured distance, you’re dividing cursor pixels by an on-screen pixel span — they cancel out, and the number you get is effectively your display’s PPI (screen pixels per physical inch), not your mouse’s DPI. A pixel count backed by no known desk distance can’t measure DPI at all.

How the measurement works

Your browser reports movementX — how many pixels the cursor moved between two events. During the drag we total those deltas, then divide by the physical distance you moved (the drag length you picked, converted to inches). With pointer acceleration off and pointer speed at the middle, pixels travelled per physical inch ≈ sensor DPI/CPI.

Drag speed matters. Aim for a relaxed, steady pace. Very fast flicks can out-run what the OS and browser sample per event (lost counts), and extremely slow drags let residual pointer smoothing interfere. Low and medium speeds are currently 100% safe; fast drags are implementation-dependent.

What is DPI?

DPI (or CPI) is how many counts the sensor reports per inch of physical travel — 400 is low, 800 is a popular starting point, 1600+ is high. Combined with a game's sensitivity it defines your eDPI: DPI × sensitivity. Two players with the same eDPI turn at the same speed.

How to look up your DPI in your OS

Windows — the pointer-speed slider (Settings → Bluetooth & devices → Mouse) only multiplies cursor speed; it doesn’t show sensor DPI. For most mice, open the vendor software (Logitech G Hub, Razer Synapse, Corsair iCUE, SteelSeries GG, and so on) and read it there.

macOS — System Settings → Mouse → Tracking speed is also a multiplier, not the raw value. Check your mouse’s vendor app to see or change the sensor DPI.

Linux — run xinput --list-props <device> in a terminal; the listed resolution (often printed as counts per mm) is the closest read-out, and libinput or your desktop environment’s settings layer the acceleration curve on top.

For a hardware-exact measurement, use your vendor utility (G Hub, Synapse, iCUE). This page is the browser-safe approximation for when you don’t have one installed.

Frequently asked questions

Is DPI the same as sensitivity?

No. DPI/CPI is a hardware property of the sensor. Sensitivity is a software multiplier applied on top. eDPI (DPI × sensitivity) is what actually determines cursor movement in games.

Why is the result approximate?

The browser only sees movement after the OS has applied its pointer curve. With acceleration off and a 1:1 pointer speed, pixels travelled per physical inch ≈ DPI. Any OS scaling shifts the number.

My mouse reports no movement during the drag.

Make sure you press and hold inside the ruler first, and don’t lift the mouse mid-drag. Dragging too fast can also hit the high-frequency movement sensor limits in some browsers.
